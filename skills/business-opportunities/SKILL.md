# Business opportunities

Building is the cheap part now. What is scarce is a problem people already spend money or time on, and a way to reach those people. This skill finds such problems and tests them in the cheapest order, so that most ideas die in days and the one that survives has evidence behind it.

The work has three steps. This skill is the hub: it holds what all steps share, plus steps 2 and 3 for now; step 1 has its own skill, `opportunity-signals`. Each step answers one question and none of them is "build the product":

| Step | Question | Who does it | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Finding | Where do people already pay, in money or time, for something that bothers them? | the agent, no contact with people | at most three candidate cards |
| 2. Verifying the problem | Do real people we can reach have this problem? | the user talks, the agent prepares | pass or fail against a criterion written beforehand |
| 3. Page or prototype with a price | Do they pay for a solution and come back to it? | the agent builds and measures, the user approves launch and budget | payments and returns |

Expect about one idea in ten to survive. The method does not find winners, it kills losers cheaply, so it only works if one test takes days, not months.

## Rules that hold in every step

- **Behaviour, not opinions.** Count what people did (paid, searched, built a workaround, came back), never what they say they would do. "I would use that" is worth nothing.
- **The stop criterion is written before the test.** After the test every number looks promising. Write "we continue only if ..." first, with a number and a date.
- **No source, no claim.** Every signal carries a link and a literal quote. A statement about a market without a source is dropped, not softened. Mark what is a found fact and what is your inference.
- **Test the riskiest assumption first**, not the most comfortable one. For small software products it is usually "can we reach these people" or "will they pay", rarely "can we build it".
- **Few candidates.** At most three per search, and no new search until one of them passes step 2 or is killed. An endless supply of plausible ideas is a way to avoid testing any.
- **Keep everything in the opportunity file** (see the end), including killed ideas and why, so a later run does not propose them again.

Know your own weaknesses as an agent in this job: you tend to return the average idea that everyone else gets, to confirm whatever hypothesis you are handed, and to write a convincing market that does not exist. The rules above and the "case against" in step 1 exist to counter that.

## Before step 1: agree where to look

Do not pick the area yourself and do not start from a product idea. Work it out with the user, then write the result into the opportunity file. Ask only what you cannot infer:

- **Who can the user reach?** Industries they worked in, communities they belong to, existing users or audience, languages they speak. Reach matters more than skill: a good problem among people the user cannot get to is not a candidate.
- **What can and will they do?** What they can build, whether they are willing to talk to customers and sell, how much time and money one test may cost.
- **What kind of customer?** Businesses, consumers, or one-person businesses and freelancers (who decide as fast as consumers but buy for work and can count whether it pays off). For a solo maker without an audience, pure consumer products are the hardest to validate cheaply.
- **Which markets and languages?**

Then propose three to five search areas and let the user choose. An area is a group of people plus a kind of source, for example "small accounting offices in <country>, through their trade forums and job ads". For each say why it could hold unsolved problems and how crowded you expect it to be.

### Look where others are not looking

Many people run the same kind of search with the same models over the same obvious sources (large English-language freelance marketplaces, software review sites, the biggest forums). What comes out of those is found by hundreds of people at once and gets cloned within weeks. A smarter agent is not an advantage, everyone has the same one. The advantage is where you look and whom the user can reach:

- **A smaller language or country**: local forums, job boards, reviews of local tools, local rules. Too small for global players, often enough for one maker. The price is a lower ceiling.
- **Unglamorous, non-technical industries**: makers search where they understand the work.
- **Public data that takes effort to process**: tenders, public contracts, registers, subsidy calls.
- **Closed places**: member-only groups, trade associations, events. An agent cannot enter, the user can. Ask them to bring back what they see.
- **The user's own data**: requests, searches and complaints from users of what they already run. Nobody else has it.
- **Recent changes**: a new legal duty, a tool that shut down or raised prices, a new platform rule. The advantage lasts weeks to months.

Use the obvious global sources mainly as a check: if the same problem is solved and paid for elsewhere, that is a good sign that money exists. A crowded source is not the same as a taken market (most people who find an idea never build it), but verify crowding for every candidate in step 1.

## Step 1: Finding

Done with the `opportunity-signals` skill: what counts as a signal and how strong it is, how to collect and group signals into problems, the five-question filter, the crowding check, the case against, and the candidate card. It needs the search area agreed above and ends with at most three candidate cards in the opportunity file.

## Step 2: Verifying the problem

Signals show that the problem exists somewhere. This step shows that it exists among people the user can actually reach.

- **Who:** 10 to 15 people from one narrow segment. Not friends, not a mix of segments.
- **Ask only about the past**, never about the future and never about your idea (this is the core of Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*):
  - When did this last happen? What did you do?
  - What did it cost you in time or money?
  - What have you tried, and why was it not enough?
  - What do you pay for today to deal with it?
- **Good signs:** they looked for a solution on their own, built a workaround, have a budget for it. **Bad signs:** "that would be nice", a problem they have to try to remember.
- **Stop criterion, written beforehand.** A rule of thumb: stop or change the segment when fewer than about a third of the people actively deal with the problem.
- **Getting the conversations is already a test of reach.** If fifty messages bring no reply, that answers question 5 of the filter.

The agent prepares the list of people, the outreach text, the question script and the evaluation. The user sends the messages and has the conversations: outreach goes out under their name, and mass automated messaging is spam. Written interviews (email, a form) work but bring fewer and thinner answers than a call.

For consumer products this step often tells little: people cannot explain why they would buy a game or a habit app, because it is taste, not a pain. There it is fair to skip to step 3 and measure behaviour.

## Step 3: A page or prototype with a price

Now describe or build a small solution that is paid for from the first day, bring people from the segment to it, and measure what they do.

| What the visitor did | What it proves |
|---|---|
| came to the page | nothing, only that the headline or ad works |
| clicked "buy" or left an email | interest, weak (costs a second, commits to nothing) |
| entered a card and paid | willingness to pay |
| paid and uses it repeatedly | that the solution works |

Most people who click "buy" never finish paying, so counting clicks overstates demand several times.

Choose the form by how long the real thing takes to build:

- **A working prototype behind a payment** when it can be built in days. One test answers both "do they pay" and "do they come back". Prefer this.
- **A page with a real pre-order or deposit** when building takes weeks or more, or the buyer is a company (there a paid pilot or a deposit is a normal way to start).
- **Doing the job by hand for money** for business customers: the customer gets the result now, and you learn exactly what to automate.
- **A page whose "buy" button leads nowhere** only as a rough sieve between several ideas, and only honestly: after the click say plainly that it is not ready and offer to leave an email. Never pretend to charge.

Rules for the test:

- **Honesty.** Say that the product is early. Take money only for something you will deliver or refund, and say so on the page.
- **Right visitors.** People must come from the segment where the signals were found. Friends and broad untargeted traffic measure the traffic, not the idea. Ways to get them: direct outreach (business customers, free but slow), communities the user is a real member of, the user's own audience, paid ads (mostly for consumer products that need hundreds of visitors). An ad is a way to bring people, not evidence.
- **Enough of them.** Only a few percent of strangers pay. With 200 visits the difference between zero and three payments is chance; plan for high hundreds to thousands of relevant visits for consumer products, or a few dozen direct offers for business customers.
- **Criterion beforehand.** Write how many visits and how many payments count as success before launching, and by which date.
- **Measurement in place before launch**: where payments and returning users will be read, with tagged links so visits can be traced to their source.

Only after this step passes does building the full product start.

## B2B and B2C

The logic is the same, the methods differ.

| | Business customers | Consumers |
|---|---|---|
| Finding signals | job ads, paid gigs, reviews of tools, new regulations | app store charts and reviews, searches, communities, what people already buy |
| Step 2 interviews | work well, 10 to 15 are enough | work poorly, often skipped |
| Who pays | often not the user of the product: verify both | the user, on impulse |
| Step 3 | deposit, paid pilot, manual service | a real payment page and measured conversion and retention |
| Sample needed | a few to a few dozen companies | hundreds to thousands of visits |
| Main risk | long sales cycle, access to the decision maker | cost of getting a customer, retention |
| Strongest proof | signed contract, renewal | still active after 30 days, renewed subscription |

One-person businesses and freelancers sit between: fast decisions, real problems with a countable value, interviews that work.

## What stays with the user

Automate the search, the research, the drafts, the building and the measurement. Keep these with the user:

- **The search area and which candidate goes to step 2.**
- **Every stop criterion**: the agent proposes, the user confirms.
- **Anything sent under their name** (outreach, community posts) and all live conversations.
- **Spending money** (ads, tools) and **taking money** (they are the seller: payment account, invoices, refunds, privacy and consumer rules).
- **The final decision to build**: the test says what passed, not whether the user wants to run this business for years.

## The opportunity file

Keep one file per search area where the user keeps project notes (ask; a natural place is `validation/<area>.md`). Start from [`opportunity-template.md`](opportunity-template.md) in this skill's folder. Read it at the start of every run, write to it at the end, and save it where it lives (commit it or hand the changes to the user): notes that stay in one session are lost. Write it in the user's language.

## Common traps

- **Starting from the idea** and looking for signals afterwards. You will always find some.
- **Counting weak signals.** A hundred complaints are worth less than five people who pay a freelancer.
- **Asking "would you pay?"** instead of asking for a payment.
- **Validating with friends**, or with whoever is easy to reach instead of the segment.
- **Reading small numbers.** Three sign-ups from forty visits is not thirty times anything.
- **Testing the build** because building is pleasant, while reach and payment stay untested.
- **Never killing anything.** If no idea has ever been stopped, the criteria are not real.
- **Endless searching.** A fourth and fifth search while the first candidates were never tested.
- **A market made of prose.** A fluent description of demand with no links and quotes behind it.

## How solid this is

The interview rules come from Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* and Steve Blank's customer development; testing demand before building from Eric Ries's *The Lean Startup* and Alberto Savoia's *The Right It*. The numbers here (a third of interviewees, 10 to 15 interviews, a few percent of visitors paying, one idea in ten) are rules of thumb from practice, not measured constants. Use them to set a criterion, and say so when a result is too small to judge.

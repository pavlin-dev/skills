# Hooks and visual pace for shorts

In the feed, the viewer decides to stay or swipe in roughly the first one or two seconds. Two failures keep repeating in agent-made shorts:

- **The first sentence is a setup, not a hook.** "This telescope had to unfold itself in space." The first three words carry nothing; the stakes only arrive in the second sentence, after three seconds.
- **The picture doesn't move enough.** A test film opened on three seconds of nearly identical frames and later held one shot for 3.5 s. The user found the background "not dynamic enough".

This skill fixes both, whatever the topic or visual style. The advice is the consensus of creator and platform-tool guides (vidIQ, Opus Clip, analyses of viral faceless shorts) and of our own test films. Most numbers in those guides are unverified and conflicting, and YouTube publishes no official thresholds, so this skill gives principles, not percentages.

## 1. The opening sentence

**Its job:** in about two seconds (5-10 spoken words), make the viewer *need* the next sentence. It does that with stakes, a result or a contradiction. It never does it with setup.

### Rules

1. **The first words do work.** Start on the strongest concrete thing: a number, a name, an object, an outcome. Don't open with "This", "So", "Today", "In this video", "Have you ever wondered", a greeting, a date, or the bare topic name.
2. **Concrete beats general.** "A 2-gram fuse" beats "a small part"; "344 steps" beats "many steps". The viewer must be able to picture it.
3. **State the stakes for someone:** what is lost, won, broken or saved.
4. **Open a loop that this video closes.** The question the hook raises must be answered inside the video. A hook the video doesn't pay off is clickbait: viewers leave later, and they remember.
5. **True.** A hook that bends a fact poisons the whole video. Check it like any other claim.
6. **One breath, the point first.** No subordinate clause or background before the point. If it runs past ~2.5 s when spoken, cut it or split it.
7. **The second sentence pays immediately.** It gives the one line of context the hook made the viewer want. It doesn't re-explain the hook.

### Hook types

Use several types when you write candidates. Story and explainer channels mostly use the first four.

| Type | Example | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome first | "This $10-billion telescope launched broken, on purpose." | explainers, stories with a known ending |
| In the middle of the action | "Twelve kilometres up, both engines went silent." | true stories, disasters, escapes |
| Stakes + count | "344 things had to go right. One failure, and it was all lost." | engineering, missions, heists |
| Contradiction | "The safest seat on a plane is the one nobody wants." | myths, science, "actually" topics |
| A specific question the video answers | "Why do planes dump fuel before they land?" | explainers; only if the answer is surprising |
| Hidden fact | "There's a lake in Tanzania so caustic it preserves dead birds like statues." | nature, history, trivia |
| Number / list | "Three mistakes that almost killed Apollo 13." | lists, how-tos, story roundups |
| Direct address | "If your Shorts die at 40% watch time, it's your first second." | tutorials, product and creator tips |

### Weak vs strong

| Weak | Why | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "This telescope had to unfold itself in space. One jam, and it was dead." | the first sentence is setup; the stakes come at second 3 | "344 things had to go right. One jam, and $10 billion was dead." |
| "In 1983, a Soviet officer named Stanislav Petrov was on duty at a bunker near Moscow." | date, name and place before any reason to care | "One man had minutes to decide whether to start a nuclear war." |
| "Today let's look at how airplanes stay in the air." | announces a topic and promises nothing | "Every airliner you've flown on can glide with all its engines off." |
| "Have you ever wondered why the sky is blue?" | a rhetorical question everyone has heard; no stakes | "The sky should be violet. Your eyes are lying to you." |

### How to pick it

1. Write the hook **first**, together with the payoff it promises, before the rest of the script.
2. Write **8-10 candidates across at least four types.** The first idea is usually the setup sentence in disguise.
3. Score each candidate:
   - Are the stakes, the result or the contradiction inside the first five words?
   - Is it concrete?
   - Is it true?
   - Does this video close the loop?
   - Can it be spoken in ≤ 2.5 s?
4. Read the best two aloud together with the second sentence and keep the one that makes the second sentence feel necessary.
5. Tell the user which candidates you considered, so they can pick another.

## 2. Three layers in the first second

The hook is not only the spoken line. In the first second three things land at once and point at the same promise:

- **Frame 0, the visual.** It is the most interesting picture of the whole video, or a promise of it: the result, the object in action, a thing mid-movement.
  - High contrast, uncluttered, readable on a phone.
  - Never a logo, a title card, an empty background, a slow establishing shot or a fade from black.
- **On-screen text.**
  - 3-7 words in the upper third, outside the platform UI, visible for at least 2 s. Most feeds autoplay muted.
  - It sharpens the promise and doesn't repeat the spoken line word for word. Spoken "344 things had to go right" pairs with the text "1 FAILURE = $10B LOST".
  - If the video has word-by-word captions, either give the first two seconds a separate title box, or show the first caption chunk larger.
- **The voice starts at once.** First word by ~0.2 s, no musical intro, no breath of silence, slightly faster and with more energy than the rest.
- **Sound:** one hit on frame 0 (impact, whoosh) if the mix has effects. Follow `sound-design` for the levels.

## 3. Motion from frame zero

Something must visibly change in the first second. A static first frame with a voice over it reads as a slideshow, and the thumb keeps moving.

- **Camera already moving:** the first frame is mid-push, mid-pan or mid-zoom, at speed, then settling.
- **The subject mid-action:** the object is already falling, launching, unfolding or breaking.
- **A reveal that has already started:** something enters the frame, splits open or grows.
- **Flash-forward:** show the end state for ~0.3-0.5 s, then snap back to the start ("this is how it ends; here's how it got there"). It pairs with an outcome-first line.
- **Scale pop:** the hero element pops in with an overshoot on the first word.

By medium:
- **Footage:** start mid-action, use punch-ins.
- **Motion graphics or cartoon:** a camera move already at speed, or an element entering the frame.
- **Screen recordings:** show the result first, then rewind.

## 4. Keep it alive: a visual-change budget

- **No stretch longer than ~3 s without a meaningful visual change** in the picture outside the caption band. Guides range from 2 to 5 s. Use 3 s for fast narration; allow up to ~5 s for a calm explainer, never more.
- **What counts as a change:**
  - a camera move (push, pan, whip, zoom through)
  - a cut or a new angle
  - a new element entering
  - a reveal or a transformation
  - a clear light or colour change
  - a b-roll insert
  - a label or kinetic text popping in (weaker; don't rely on it alone)
- **What does not count:** boiling or wobbling lines, grain, a slow drift of a few percent, and caption changes. Captions sit on top of the picture; they don't make the picture dynamic.
- **Tie every change to the narration.** The change lands on the word it illustrates. Changes for their own sake become predictable, and predictable changes stop working.
- **Vary the kind of change:** not a zoom every 2 s, but a zoom, then a new element, then a camera travel, then a cut closer.
- **Escalate:** bigger changes at the turns of the story, the biggest at the payoff.
- **Fixing a still stretch:**
  - let the camera travel to the next thing
  - move or transform the subject
  - animate the comparison instead of showing it
  - add a secondary motion (particles, background traffic, weather)
  - cut closer
- **Change is not the same as quality.** The first version of the test film showed separate drawings with crossfades between them. It passed the change audit, yet the user found it disconnected. The second version, one continuous world, felt much better, but it had the static opening and a 3.5 s still stretch. You need both: continuity (the style skills, e.g. `cartoon-world-video`, `motion-design-video`) and enough change (this skill).

## 5. The end and the loop

- End on the payoff. No outro, no "subscribe", no logo card.
- Optionally make the end flow into the start, so the replay feels seamless: the last frame matches frame 0, or the last line sets up the first. A replay counts as watch time, but don't force a loop that makes the ending weaker.

## 6. Check it

- **The line.** Count the words before the stakes (≤ 5) and the length of the hook when spoken (≤ ~2.5 s). From a word timeline (the `voice-over` skill's `timeline.json`), read the time of the first stakes word.
- **The render:** run `python3 scripts/motion_audit.py out.mp4 --sheet hook.png` (needs ffmpeg).
  - It prints an activity strip per second, every still stretch longer than `--max-still` (default 3 s), and whether the first second moves.
  - Fix every still stretch it lists.
  - Look at `hook.png` (the first 3 s, every 0.25 s): is frame 0 interesting, does the picture change across the first four frames, is the hook text readable?
- **After publishing:** tell the user to watch "Viewed vs. swiped away" in YouTube Studio. YouTube gives no benchmark, so compare with the channel's own shorts.
  - A low rate means the hook is the problem.
  - A high rate with low average percentage viewed means the body didn't keep the hook's promise.

## Before sending

- The hook was chosen from several written candidates. Its stakes, result or contradiction sit in the first five words, it is true, and the video pays it off.
- Frame 0 is the most interesting or most promising picture, not a title, logo or empty frame; the picture moves within the first second.
- The voice starts at ~0.2 s; the hook text sits in the upper third for at least 2 s.
- `motion_audit.py` lists no still stretch longer than ~3 s (or 5 s for a calm explainer), and every change is tied to the narration.
- The ending is the payoff, with no outro.
